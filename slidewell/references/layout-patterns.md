# Layout pattern library

**Covers:** which layout each slide should use for the job it does, and how each pattern is constructed.

**Doesn't cover:** finishing rules that apply to every layout (`finishing-standard.md`) or chart rules (`charts.md`).

Read this when storyboarding (to choose how each slide will prove its headline) and when building (for how each pattern is constructed). Pick the pattern that fits the slide's *job*, not the one that looks most impressive. One idea per slide; the pattern exists to make that idea obvious.

## A. Choosing a pattern by job

| The slide needs to… | Consider |
|---|---|
| Orient the audience | Agenda tracker, exec-summary tracker, section divider, breadcrumb |
| Make a recommendation with reasons | Parallel pillars, numbered support boxes, label column + bullets |
| Land one or a few numbers | Hero numbers, show-the-math chain |
| Prove a claim with data | Annotated chart, highlighted series, waterfall |
| Compare options | Criteria tick table, RAG matrix, X-vs-Y, pros/cons per option |
| Explain a process or plan | Numbered method with active step, phased chevrons, timeline, Gantt, roadmap table |
| Explain who does what | Org/governance chart, swimlane, role-family table, hub-and-spoke |
| Define scope | Value chain with scope highlighted, spectrum-of-options matrix |
| Get a decision | Decision bar, agenda with decisions, next-steps tiles |
| Get the room talking or working | Question-to-the-room, exercise, then capture, brainstorm box, trade-off with questions |
| Win work | Understanding, phased offer (core + options), pricing options, deliverable thumbnails, team, impact numbers, logo wall, case study, client quote |
| Describe customers or concepts | Persona, segmentation columns with photos, concept mood board, parallel concept sequences |

## B. Navigation and framing

**Breadcrumb.** A short, all-caps topic label (usually 1–3 words: "DEMAND", "TIMETABLE", "DECISIONS", "QUESTION 1: WHERE ARE WE NOW?") naming where the slide sits. **It is never narrative:** the message belongs in the headline, not the breadcrumb. Use "SIDEBAR: …" for a deliberate detour. Same position, size, and colour on every slide, and large enough to read (a small grey label above the headline only works if it's made clearly bigger).

**Navigation style (ask the user).** Options: a breadcrumb tag or label above the headline (default); a small section tracker centred along the bottom of the slide; a mini version of the deck's framework or diagram with the current part highlighted; a rail down the left edge (use sparingly, as it can look gimmicky).

**Agenda tracker.** The agenda reappears at the start of each section with the current item emphasized (larger, brand color) and the others muted (grey or low opacity). For a timed session, draw it as a horizontal timeline with start and end times and numbered stops. Muted rows use a lighter fill and lighter text, never a white box with an outline.

**Exec summary as tracker** (label the repeats "Contents"; see finishing-standard §10). For findings decks, the executive summary is a column of colored labels (one per section) with supporting bullets. The labels are mini findings ("Profitable stores"), not topic words ("Economics"); see finishing-standard §10. Each section divider reuses it with the current row highlighted and the rest faded. This reminds the audience of the whole argument at every transition.

**Section divider.** Full-bleed brand color or image, section number + short label. Labels are allowed here instead of message headlines.

**Numbered divider with edge motif.** A large section numeral ("01") above the section name on a brand-colour panel, with a photo filling the rest of the slide, divided by the deck's edge motif (a wave, a slant, a curve). Every divider uses the same panel width, numeral size, and edge; only the number, name, and photo change. A recurring hero image cropped the same way on every divider works as well.

**Objectives bookends (workshops).** Open with "At the end of this session, you should: …". Close with the same list ("At the beginning, this is what we said we'd cover"), then a version filled in with what was actually covered.

**Two-tier title.** A message headline in brand color, with a one-line grey subtitle beneath that names the frame, principle, or scope ("Design principle: inefficiencies driven by extra reporting layers"). Useful when a series of slides applies different lenses to the same evidence.

## C. Argument structures

**Parallel pillars.** Three (sometimes four) columns, each with a number or icon badge, a short kicker in accent color ("Can afford to…"), a claim in the main color, one hero number with a label, and two or three short bullets. Footnotes carry qualifiers. Every column has identical geometry and type styles.

**Hero numbers.** Two to four large figures in tiles, each with a short label beneath ("48: stakeholder interviews"). Use for scale or credibility, not as decoration. Mark estimates (e.g., "30+", "~", or an asterisk with a footnote).

**Label column + bullets.** A narrow column of colored label boxes on the left (the groupings), with the supporting points to the right. This is the pyramid made visible.

**Badges on containers.** Number or icon badges sit centred on a container's left edge or top-left corner (the corner at the badge's centre), not inside the box. This frees space for larger text and makes the layout less boxed-in; leave enough gap between containers that a badge never touches its neighbour.

**Numbered support boxes.** A stack of numbered rows, each with a bold lead phrase and one or two lines of support. Bold the key phrase within each bullet.

**Quote slide.** A single sourced quote, large, with key words in brand color, and the citation in the source position. Pull-quote boxes can sit beside a diagram to show external backing.

**X vs Y.** Two options side by side with a "VS" (or "OR") marker between them, each listing its distinguishing features. For strategic trade-offs, add a "Points of tension" box and a "Questions to consider" box.

## D. Analytics

**Annotated chart.** The headline states the conclusion; short accent-colored annotations with thin leader lines or circles point to the exact marks that prove it. Annotations are native, editable text even if the chart itself is an image.

**Highlighted series.** One bar or series in the accent color, the rest muted, so the eye goes to the point the headline makes.

**Waterfall.** From a starting total through each contributing driver to the result. Good for savings, budgets, and bridges between two figures.

**Show-the-math chain.** A vertical sequence of bold figures joined by downward arrows (e.g., population → affected subset → cost → net saving), each with its assumption in small grey text beneath. Use it whenever a headline number would otherwise be taken on trust. Construction: all figures in a column share one centre line, with the markers on that same line; each figure is vertically centred on the label-and-note pair it belongs to; markers are small downward triangles or short thin arrows; any divider between columns is thin and in the headline colour.

**Gap chart.** Current vs future state (e.g., activities owned today vs required), with the gap called out.

**Criteria tick table.** Options as columns, criteria as rows, with ticks or Harvey balls. Group criteria under headings. Highlight the option or criterion currently under discussion.

**RAG matrix.** Options × criteria scored Green/Amber/Red with a legend explaining each rating, plus an overall column and a recommendation column. Never rely on red/green alone: put the word or a symbol in the cell too.

**Budget and cost.** Stacked bars by year for cost composition; a waterfall for net cost or income; a compact table for staff profile or phasing. Totals are always visible.

**Charts from specialist tools.** Build charts natively wherever practical, so they can be restyled. When a visual can't reasonably be rebuilt (e.g., sunbursts, org heatmaps exported from an analytics tool), place it as an image and keep the title, annotations, and source native.

## E. Process, structure, and planning diagrams

**Numbered method with active step.** A vertical list of numbered steps (e.g., a six-step appraisal method), shown once in full, then repeated as a tracker on later slides with the current step highlighted and its detail alongside.

**Building diagram (n of N).** A framework built up across consecutive slides, each adding one element ("…asking a series of questions (3/6)"), ending with the complete picture. This is how to do "animation" in a static deck.

**Phased chevrons.** Three or four chevrons or boxes for phases, each with a title and short description; the current phase is outlined (e.g., dashed accent border) and labeled "Current focus".

**Timeline.** A horizontal line with milestones alternating above and below, dates bold, and "today" or "next" marked. A past-to-future timeline can lead into a forward plan. Each label connects to its exact point with a short thin arrow that stops just short of the marker; labels for a span sit centred above that span. Keep markers small and only where something happens. Give the line a deliberate weight (clearly thin or clearly bold). A timeline running into the future ends in an arrowhead, chevrons, or hatching, or runs off the right edge when it continues on the next slide (entering from the left there).

**Gantt / work plan.** Workstreams as rows, months as columns, bars for activities, a legend for status, and an optional "today" line. Keep labels readable; split across slides rather than shrinking.

**Roadmap table.** Short / medium / long term as column headers (chevron-styled), with dimensions as rows (model, activity, governance, funding, staffing…). Good for showing how an arrangement evolves.

**Value chain with scope.** A row of chevrons for end-to-end activities (e.g., strategy → engagement → marketing → sales → finance → delivery) with activities listed beneath, and the in-scope portion boxed or highlighted.

**Spectrum of options.** Dimensions as rows; along each row, pill-shaped options arranged from one extreme to the other; selected or in-scope pills filled, others outlined; a legend explains the fill states.

**Long-list tree.** Broad categories on the left branching to a numbered list of specific options on the right, with notes. Pairs with a filter step that narrows to a short list.

**Org / governance chart.** Tiers (e.g., advisory, executive, delivery) as labeled bands, boxes for bodies or roles, solid vs dashed lines for reporting vs advisory relationships.

**Swimlane process.** Actors as horizontal lanes, steps as boxes, arrows across lanes, with a legend for step types.

**Hub-and-spoke / cycle.** A central idea with surrounding elements (benefits, stakeholder groups, a repeating cycle). Keep it to six or fewer spokes.

**Models with icons.** Three parallel model types (e.g., activity / operating / transaction models), each with a definition line and small schematic icons; the one under discussion boxed as "Focus of this appraisal".

**Role-family table.** Function | example role titles | key activities.

## F. Decisions and status

**Decision bar.** A filled bar at the bottom of a slide stating the decision sought ("Decision: Do we agree to support continued development of the programme?"). For steering and executive audiences, every discussion slide should end in one.

**Decision tag.** A small box in the top-right corner naming the decision this slide feeds ("Decision: Confirm next steps"), useful when the slide is evidence rather than the ask itself.

**Agenda with decisions.** Each agenda item paired with the decision it should produce.

**Actions table.** Action | owner | date raised | status; status color-coded with words, not color alone.

**Next-steps tiles.** Month tiles down the left (NOV, DEC, FEB…) with the milestone for each.

**Status markers.** Use these honestly so drafts and demos aren't mistaken for final results:
- "Work in progress" or "WIP" stamp on unfinished slides
- "Version 1 / Version 2" tags when showing alternatives
- "Illustrative" corner banner and "[DUMMY DATA]" in the subtitle for example data
- An assumptions slide whenever figures depend on assumptions

**Cross-reference note.** A small sticky-note-style box ("See slide 12 for the full option list") pointing to supporting detail elsewhere.

## G. Facilitation

Every facilitation slide must make clear what the room is doing while it's on screen: listening, discussing, deciding, brainstorming, or answering.

**Icebreaker template.** A fill-in-the-blanks script with the blanks in brand color ("I'm [name]… [role] from [location]… and to treat myself I like to [something most people don't know]."). Optionally followed by a capture slide of answers.

**Norms.** "We will: …" (e.g., be present; interact; "yes, and…"; vent constructively), paired with a speech-bubble question ("What will help us make the most of our time together?").

**Question to the room.** A full-bleed brand-color slide with one large question ("Do we have any skeptics in the room?") and a smaller parenthetical prompt. The visual change signals it's the audience's turn.

**Teach, then apply, then capture.** Explain a tool (e.g., a SWOT), show a blank version with prompt questions in each part, then one slide per part with the room's input, plus a mini version of the tool in the corner showing which part is active.

**Exercise, then capture.** A prompt slide ("Let's futurecast: it's 2032, what are they announcing about you?"), then a slide for the group's answers ("What are your headlines?").

**Brainstorm box.** A labeled box ("TO BRAINSTORM") containing the question, placed beside the content it relates to.

**Trade-off slide.** "Strategic trade-off: X OR Y", with "Points of tension" (evidence on each side) and "Questions to consider".

**Reveal pairs.** When teaching, a question slide ("What is your reaction to this list?") followed by the answer slide. The one sanctioned exception to answer-first.

**Live vs share versions.** The live version can withhold answers and build up step by step. The share version, sent afterward, keeps everything static and adds the session's contributions in a distinct highlight color (e.g., blue text), so readers can see what came from the room.

**Builds.** Show progressive reveals as successive slides (see "Building diagram"), not PowerPoint animations, so every state is visible, printable, and editable.

## I. Proposals and credentials

**Understanding.** Opens the proposal with the client's situation, ambitions, and what success looks like for them, in their words: three columns (context and opportunity, scope of work, outcomes), each headed by a photo strip or icon. The headline restates their goal, not your firm.

**Phased offer (core + options).** The recommended core scope as one strong tile (brand colour, icon, name, one-line description, duration), followed by optional stages in a lighter tint marked "Optional", in time order. A photo mosaic can sit between the tiles. The headline says why this structure helps the client ("lets you start small and extend").

**Delivery plan.** A Gantt of phases and sprints: phase bands across the top (core in the main colour, options in tints), workstreams as rows, activities as bars; a legend for the activity types. Show the same phase colours as the phased-offer slide.

**Pricing options.** Two or three cards side by side ("Regular", "Light"), each with an icon, the team mix, the cost per sprint, and the indicative total, with identical structure so they compare at a glance; assumptions in a side panel (a photo panel with an overlay works well); any discount stated plainly. Never more options than the client can choose between.

**Example deliverables.** A grid of four to six thumbnail cards, each a small picture of a real output type (a dashboard, a model, a process map) under a coloured title bar, so the client can see what they'll get.

**Team.** Headshots in identical crops, name, role, and one line on what each person brings; or an org chart with headshots for the core team. Biographies, if needed, go in an appendix slide with a consistent block per person.

**Impact numbers (credentials).** A grid of six to nine big numbers from past work ("£25M", "450 tonnes", "16%"), each with its unit in a contrasting accent colour and a one-line caption, beside a dark panel with one sentence on why these results matter. Numbers must be real and sourced from the user.

**Logo wall.** Client or partner logos in a regular grid, scaled to equal visual weight, all full colour or all one tone, under a headline that says something ("We have worked with a variety of large and small clients across sectors").

**Case study.** One slide per case: a column of three labelled blocks (the client, the challenge, the solution) each with an icon, and an outcomes panel (a numbered list over a photo with an overlay, or on a brand colour), ending with a short client quote if there is one.

**Client quote / testimonial.** A portrait in a frame, name and role, the quote in the main text size with a large quote mark in the brand colour, and the logos of both organisations. Use only real quotes, with permission.

## J. Customers, concepts, and imagery

**Persona.** A circular photo of a representative customer on a brand-colour panel, with simple slider bars for two or three behaviours (brand loyalty, price sensitivity), and four icon-led blocks: way of living, passions and interests, influences, pain points and concerns. The subtitle is a one-line portrait of the person.

**Segmentation columns.** Three to five equal columns, each headed by a coloured label and a photo of that segment, with four or five short bullets underneath. Same bullet count and order of topics in every column, so segments compare across.

**Concept mood board.** For a brand or creative concept: the big idea, story, and benefits in icon-led columns across the top; a mosaic of photos and brand-colour tiles carrying one word each ("Traditional", "Recognisable") across the bottom.

**Photo panel with statement.** A photo strip on one side of a content slide with a short statement on an overlay (two or three sentences on what the organisation believes, e.g. "Our way of working"), balancing an icon grid or bullets on the other side.

**Comparing alternatives.** When a deck presents two or more options or concepts in turn, each gets the **same sequence of slides with identical layouts** (e.g. strategic focus → target customer → concept, for each concept). The audience learns the layout once, then compares the content. Mark which alternative each slide belongs to in the breadcrumb or title ("Concept 1: Tribute").

## H. Content-density benchmark

The target for a standalone-but-presentable slide is: one message headline; three parallel pillars, each with a kicker, a claim, one hero number, and about three short bullets; qualifiers in footnotes. It can be read without a presenter, but isn't so dense that it can *only* be read. When a slide carries much more than this, split it or move detail to an appendix; when it carries much less, check it still proves its headline.
