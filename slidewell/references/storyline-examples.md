# Storyline examples

**Covers:** the principles in `storytelling.md` shown at work: strong and weak headline sequences, the flow check, weak headline patterns and when they're fine, grouping in practice, workshop arcs, and the exact storyline proposal format to send the user.

**Doesn't cover:** the principles themselves (`storytelling.md`).

> All organisations, people, places, and figures in these examples are invented. The patterns are taken from real consulting and workshop decks.

Read this alongside `storytelling.md` when drafting a storyline. It shows what strong headlines, sequences, and sections look like, the weak patterns to rewrite, and the exact format of the storyline proposal to send the user.

## 1. What a strong headline sequence looks like

Read only the headlines. A strong deck still makes its full argument, and **each headline leads naturally to the next**. Structure (sections, trackers) helps the audience navigate, but it's the slide-to-slide flow, plus specific, concrete individual headlines, that makes a storyline persuasive.

### Example A: investment pitch (the model for flow)

Each headline raises a question that the next one answers. The link between each pair is shown in brackets; it isn't on the slides, but it's what the audience feels.

1. We suggest offering $300K debt financing to Kelpwise, a seaweed-packaging startup based in Portugal
   *(why?)*
2. Every year, >90,000 tons of single-use food packaging end up in Portugal's coastal waters
   *(so what?)*
3. This packaging waste is damaging fisheries, tourism income, and marine habitats
   *(what would fix it?)*
4. To solve this issue, packaging needs a compostable alternative that costs the same as plastic
   *(who can do that?)*
5. Kelpwise is the only company in the region to grow, process, and sell seaweed packaging end to end
   *(what difference would it make?)*
6. Kelpwise has the potential to deliver significant environmental and economic impact along the coast
   *(can they deliver it?)*
7. Kelpwise's leadership team has the commitment and expertise to scale across Portugal and into Spain
   *(and also…)*
8. Kelpwise has also signed supply partnerships that will bolster its go-to-market and revenue
   *(therefore…)*
9. We recommend a $300K debt investment in Kelpwise as part of its $1.5M Seed Round
   *(what would it achieve?)*
10. Kelpwise's seed funding would return tangible results
    *(but what about the risk?)*
11. While this investment is not risk-free, we believe risks can be mitigated

*Why it works:*
- **Slide-to-slide narrative:** it opens with the ask, then runs situation (2) → complication (3) → question (4) → answer (5), and keeps going: each slide states something, the next draws out its consequence or the obstacle it creates, and the next resolves it. You could join any two consecutive headlines with "because", "so", "but" or "therefore".
- **Specific, concrete headlines:** real figures (>90,000 tons, $300K, $1.5M), named actors, and claims that can be checked ("the only company in the region to grow, process, and sell end to end"), not generic statements.
- **Objections answered before they're asked:** capability (7), support (8), and risk (11).
- The section structure (problem, solution, investment) and the returning tracker help, but they're not why it persuades.

### Example B: good headlines, weaker flow

1. A fast-moving drone-inspection industry offers attractive investment opportunities
2. Inspection drones have evolved from manually flown cameras into autonomous, AI-guided survey tools
3. Inspection job costs are falling as both hardware and software get cheaper
4. Better capability and lower costs will expand the market under all forecast scenarios
5. Venture capital funding has picked up as a result

*What's good:* each headline is one clear, specific claim. *What's weaker:* the transitions. 2 → 3 jumps from capability to cost with nothing linking them, and 1 → 2 starts a history lesson without saying why it matters to the investor. Small bridges fix it (illustrative rewrite):
1. Drone inspection offers attractive investment opportunities, *because two trends are converging*
2. Inspection drones have evolved from manually flown cameras into autonomous, AI-guided survey tools
3. *At the same time*, the cost of an inspection job is falling as hardware and software get cheaper
4. *Together*, better capability at lower cost will expand the market under every forecast scenario
5. *Investors have noticed*: venture capital funding has picked up as a result

### Example C: a parallel list (useful, but not a narrative)

- There is an opportunity to save a further £40–50M in the engineering workforce
- Example Opportunity 1: Achieve £2.1M savings by removing a management layer
- Example Opportunity 2: Achieve £1.6M savings by increasing team sizes
- Example Opportunity 3: Achieve £1.1M savings by aligning pay bands
- Example Opportunity 4: Achieve £800K savings by relying less on long-term contractors
- More opportunities for efficiency could be identified with further data

This is simply a list of comparable options. When the slide job is to enumerate (opportunities, options, recommendations), a consistent headline form ("Achieve £X by [action]") and a number in every headline make the items easy to compare. But don't mistake a list for a storyline: the persuasion has to come from the slides around it (here, the executive summary and the closing next step).

### Example D: proposal to set something up (the case for action)

1. *[Title]* Proposal: a Social Impact Club at Purbeck University
2. There is strong demand for a social impact club *(people want it: survey of 61 students, plus clubs at 9 peer universities)*
3. The club will help students put their skills to work for local charities, and bring charities' experience onto campus *(here's what it's for)*
4. We would run a mix of volunteering projects, speaker events, and skills workshops to deliver on the club's mission *(here's how)*
5. The club will work closely with the university's careers and volunteering teams rather than duplicate them *(how it fits with what exists)*
6. The club will run lean and is here to stay to meet the strong demand *(how it will be managed, financially and relationally)*

*Why it works:* a classic case for action: demand → precedent → purpose → activities → fit with existing structures → management and finances. Each slide pre-empts the next objection a decision-maker would raise (is it wanted? what will it do? will it duplicate others? can it sustain itself?). Built for a short slot, each slide does a little more work than in a workshop, but none is packed with text.

### Example E: recommendation that weighs options

1. Aerovant should enter a 50/50 JV with Supplier A for landing-gear design (pushing for domestic manufacturing) *(exec summary)*
2. Landing-gear design and project management should be brought closer to Aerovant to fix current delays
3. There are benefits and risks to full vertical integration
4. *Therefore*, a joint venture may be the most efficient solution
5. We propose entering into a 50/50 JV with Supplier A
6. This JV offers Aerovant a positive NPV of ~$300M, not including benefits from customer satisfaction
7. *On balance*, we also recommend domestic production to prioritize landing-gear quality
8. Aerovant will need to prepare for a strong industry response

*Why it works:* the problem (2) leads to the obvious fix and its trade-offs (3); "Therefore" resolves the trade-off into the recommended option (4–5); the value case follows (6); a secondary decision is weighed and resolved with "On balance" (7); the close anticipates the reaction (8). Connectors sit exactly at the turning points. (Slide 3 of this deck is a typical "there are benefits and risks" set-up headline, acceptable because the next slide resolves it.)

### Example F: update to a steering group (progress and decisions)

1. The programme is on track for April go-live, and two decisions are needed today *(status at a glance)*
2. Recruitment is nearly complete: 11 of 12 adviser posts are filled, and the last starts in March
3. Pilot sites have supported 140 businesses since October, ahead of the 120 target
4. Two risks have risen since last month: the data-sharing agreement is late, and one site has lost its lead
5. Decision 1: Approve a six-week extension to the data-sharing workstream *(decision bar)*
6. Decision 2: Agree a temporary lead for the northern site from February *(decision bar)*
7. Next steps before the March meeting

*Why it works:* it opens with the status and the asks, so the group knows from slide 1 what it must do. The standing items (recruitment, delivery, risks) keep the same order every month, which the audience expects, but each one states what changed rather than just naming the topic. Each decision gets its own slide and decision bar, so none gets lost in the update. See storytelling §5b for the update deck type.

### 1b. Checking how the headlines flow

Run this on every storyline before sending it to the user (and again on the built deck):

1. **Read the headlines aloud, in order, with nothing else.** Does it sound like one argument or like a list of topics?
2. **Name the link between each consecutive pair**: *because*, *so*, *but*, *therefore*, *as a result*, *which means*, *for example*, *at the same time*, *and also*. If no link word fits naturally, the flow breaks there: reorder, add a bridging slide, or rewrite one of the headlines.
3. **Check that each headline answers the question the previous one raises** (why? so what? how? who? what would it take? what about the risk?). If a headline answers a question nobody asked yet, it's in the wrong place.
4. **Check the joins between sections** as carefully as within them: the last headline of one section should set up the first of the next.
5. **Look for the SCQA arc** at deck level and, where it helps, inside sections (as in Example A, slides 2–5).
6. **Anticipate objections:** where would this audience push back? Put the answer at that point in the sequence.
7. **Make links explicit only where they help.** Connectors ("However", "As a result", "At the same time") are useful at turning points; you don't need one on every headline.

**Techniques visible across these decks**
- **Number in the headline** when the number *is* the point ("£40–50M", "~90% of surveyed customers…").
- **Connectors that carry the logic between slides**: "However, …", "…as well as…", "Additionally, …", "As a result", "This keeps…", "One caveat…", "Because of these factors, …", "But we believe…", "But it won't be easy…". Use them at turning points where consecutive slides build on each other (see §1b).
- **Tension headlines for trade-offs**: "Staying focused on repairs preserves margin, but may cap long-term growth." *[X] preserves/gains A, but risks B.*
- **Objection handling**: "While this investment is not risk-free, we believe risks can be mitigated."
- **Forward-looking close**: "What will success look like in 12 months' time?"

## 2. Weak headline patterns and how to fix them

Each pattern below appears in real working decks. They're worth avoiding when **you** generate slides, because each one hands the work of interpretation to the audience. But they aren't always mistakes: when **revising a user's deck**, point them out only where they weaken the story, and don't strip them out by default, since the user may want them.

| Pattern | Examples seen | Why it's weak | When it's fine | Fix otherwise: say what you found or what to do |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Topic label** | "Updates", "Operating Model", "Unit economics", "Legal Considerations", "Stakeholder engagement" | Says what the slide is *about*, not what it *says* (an intellectually blank statement) | Slides with little else on them where the label is the content (dividers, a single quote or definition, agenda, contact); method slides; and table or chart titles that sit *below* the real headline (e.g. "Current ticket prices" labelling a table) | "Unit economics: each tonne of packaging sold earns ~$150–250 margin after shipping" |
| **Process narration** | "We conducted a brand associations exercise to identify…", "We identified a variety of options for the long-list", "We assessed profitability drivers via…" | Describes the work, not the result | Slides about process, method, or project structure. Not for recommendation slides, not as the dominant style through the deck, and never as the style of executive-summary bullets | "Customers associate the brand mainly with friendly, local service" |
| **Colon cliffhanger** | "We believe the key is to:", "We are aware of the need to consider broader factors, including:" | The message is in the body, so the headline sequence breaks | Facilitation slides where holding the answer helps the session flow, and consecutive slides that are part A and part B of one point (the next headline completes the thought) | State the key itself: "The key is to shift from selling tickets to building a membership" |
| **"There are several…"** | "There are several options for a go-live timeframe", "There are various ways [X] can bring these values to life", "Within each operating model, there are various options to consider" | A count without a claim | Sometimes needed: when the slide's job is to introduce a set of options or a choice that the following slides (or the body) resolve | Name the recommendation or the deciding factor: "Launching in January avoids the election period and gives three months to recruit" |
| **Two ideas in one** | "We are working to a tight comms timeline, and waiting for the Steering Group (14 Mar) to confirm detailed plans" | Splits attention; usually >2 lines | Rarely | Keep the one that matters; move the other to the body |
| **Question as headline** | Long criterion headlines in question form ("Criterion 4: Affordability – How will an option be financed and is it affordable…?") | Asks instead of answers | When citing questions that belong to a framework or method (e.g. assessment criteria, "Where are we now?"); in workshops, to build suspense, especially on a light slide whose job is to tee up the next one; exercise and capture slides; questions to the room | Answer it: "Only the in-house and joint-unit options are affordable within existing budgets" |
| **Duplicate headline, different content** | Same headline on two consecutive slides with different evidence | Readers assume it's a repeat | Build sequences (n/N), trackers, and option sets (Option A/B/C) | Otherwise each slide gets its own claim |
| **Typos and grammar** | "FFinal Report", "this questions", "What is / could be stopping us close the gap" | Undermines credibility immediately | Never | Spell-check every headline; read the sequence aloud |

These patterns are defaults, not absolutes: use the "When it's fine" column, and when you use an exception, make sure it's deliberate. Status-report decks may use short labels for standing items, but their discussion slides still need message headlines.

## 3. Well-grouped sections

Grouping and ordering are two separate decisions. First decide **what belongs together** (grouping), then **what order to put it in** (ordering). Both apply at every level: sections in a deck, slides in a section, boxes or bullets on a slide.

The grouping tests (same kind of idea, same level, summarized by its parent, MECE) and the ordering options (importance, chronology, short → long term, strategy → operations, internal → external, geographic, deductive, problem → solution → ask, general → specific) are in `storytelling.md` §3. This section shows them at work.

### Examples from real decks

**MECE value levers with memorable names.** A museum turnaround deck groups every recommendation under three sections, each opened by a divider:
- *Creating Reasons to Visit* (exhibitions, events, facilities)
- *Connecting with Audiences* (marketing, partnerships)
- *Capturing Visitor Value* (pricing, membership, hours, café and shop)

Product, promotion, and price don't overlap, together cover the revenue problem, and the parallel wording makes them easy to remember.

**Problem / solution / ask.** The investment pitch (Example A) uses the SCQA arc at deck level: the problem is the situation and complication, the company is the answer, and the investment is the ask.

**Geographic ordering within a section.** "Locally, there are opportunities to partner with schools and community groups" → "Regionally, … collaboration and coalition-building" → "Nationally, … engage with membership associations". Local → regional → national is an ordering the audience grasps instantly.

**Time-horizon ordering.** "We recommend a mix of quick (front-of-house) and longer-term (gallery) changes", and phased roadmaps ("Short term → Medium term → Long term") let the audience see what happens now and what comes later.

**Decision-led steering packs.** "There are three key decisions which are related but can be made separately", followed by one slide per decision (senior recruitment, go-live date, name), each ending in a decision bar. The grouping follows what the meeting must decide, not the order the work was done.

**Main deck vs annex.** Long steering packs keep the decision story to ~12 slides and move background (scope detail, organisation structure, financial model, legal advice, option appraisal) into lettered annexes (Annex A–G), each with its own divider. The main deck stays readable; nothing is lost.

**Weak grouping to avoid**
- Sections named by work stream or meeting habit ("Updates", "Operating Model", "AOB") with no claim. Fine for a weekly status call, weak for a decision meeting.
- Several slides in a row with the same label headline (e.g., three slides headed "OPERATING MODEL"): the section has a name but no argument.
- Mixing levels: a section whose slides alternate between recommendations and background evidence without signalling which is which. Use a "RECOMMENDATION" breadcrumb (as the museum deck does) or move evidence to the annex.

## 4. Workshop storylines

There's no single right arc for a workshop or talk; two are shown below. A strong workshop deck is organized around **what the room will do**, not just what the facilitator will say. The strategic-planning workshop shows the arc:

1. **Open:** title → introductions (icebreaker template, then captured answers) → agenda with times → objectives → norms
2. **Section 1: Why:** a question to the room ("Do we have any skeptics in the room?") → reasons to be skeptical vs. "But:" → definitions from credible sources
3. **Section 2: How:** a six-question process, revealed one question at a time ("…asking a series of questions (1/6)" … "(6/6)"), each question with its tool (SWOT, futurecasting, issue tree, PESTLE, SMART) and an exercise, with a SIDEBAR for a detour (issue trees)
4. **Section 3: What this means for us:** concrete message headlines ("After 25 years, Brightwater Learning is ready for its first complete strategic plan", "Next, a six-month process will produce a five-year plan for 2028–2033"), then questions to the room ("What are your first reactions?", "How do you want to be involved?")
5. **Close:** objectives revisited → "We've covered it!" with what was learned → thanks and feedback

*What makes it work:* the breadcrumb tracks the room's place in the process (SECTION 2 → QUESTION 3: HOW DO WE GET THERE?); teaching slides state a message even when the body is interactive; the last section turns the general method into a specific plan and a specific ask.

**Alternative arc: an explainer talk** (spoken, relaxed, engaging). A briefing on a national health system for an audience from another country:
1. **Hook:** the audience's own system on screen, with its strengths, then (on click) its weaknesses
2. **Why listen:** three reasons in big numbered tiles
3. **Reveal:** the brand logo full-screen; "What *is* it?" → definitions crossed out one by one → "Yes… but what *is* it?" → "It's complicated!"
4. **How it works:** one simple comparison of the two systems; "What should you take away from this?" (two numbered takeaways)
5. **Half time**
6. **Three lessons**, one per slide with a numbered header ("More choice doesn't always mean better care", "More money doesn't mean fewer problems", "Anything can be changed, and anything can go wrong"), then two takeaways
7. **"Otherwise… we're under water"** (what happens if nothing changes) → **Question time**
8. **Reference pack:** dense, readable slides (structure, history, funding, comparisons, resources) for afterwards

*What makes it work:* questions and humour create momentum; the spoken part is light on text; the takeaways are repeated in numbered tiles; the detail is kept but moved to the back (see visual-identity §5–6).

Workshop-specific headline rules:
- Teaching slides still get message headlines ("To be helpful, your objectives should be SMART").
- Capture and exercise slides may have a question as the headline ("What are your headlines?"), with the big question in the body if needed.
- Build sequences repeat the headline with a counter (n/N); nothing else repeats.

## 5. The storyline proposal format

Send this in the conversation (not as a deck) at the storyline gate.

```
GOVERNING MESSAGE
<one sentence: the answer, recommendation, or request>

OPENING (SCQA)
S: <undisputed context>   C: <what's changed / the problem>
Q: <the question this deck answers>   A: <the governing message>

STORYLINE
Section 1: <section name as a claim or clear label>
  1. <headline>  | proves via: <pattern/chart + evidence> | job: present / discuss / decide / exercise
  2. …
Section 2: …
Appendix: <slides moved out of the main story>

BUILDING BLOCKS: <from the intake menu>
ASSUMPTIONS / GAPS: <what you assumed; what evidence is missing for which headline>
```

**Worked example (fictional)**

```
GOVERNING MESSAGE
A demand-led timetable would let the same staff support ~20% more visitors.

OPENING (SCQA)
S: Visits have recovered to 2019 levels.
C: But 62% of visits now happen after 4pm, when staffing is at its lowest.
Q: How should the library rearrange its hours?   A: As above.

STORYLINE
Section 1: Demand has moved to evenings
  1. 62% of visits now happen after 4pm, up from 35% in 2019 | proves via: 100% stacked column by time band, 2019 vs 2025 | job: present
  2. Most evening visitors are students who come to study | proves via: sorted horizontal bar of visit purpose, study highlighted | job: present
Section 2: A new timetable fits staff to visitors
  3. Moving two daytime shifts into the evening would cover peak demand in full | proves via: visitors per hour vs staff capacity, today and new | job: present
  4. The change is cost-neutral and lets staff support ~20% more visitors | proves via: show-the-math chains (cost; visitors supported) | job: present
Section 3: Two decisions for the board
  5. Approve a six-month pilot of the new timetable from March | proves via: pilot timeline + decision bar | job: decide
  6. Agree three measures of success before the pilot starts | proves via: three KPI tiles with targets + discussion bar | job: discuss
Appendix: We combined door counts, a visitor survey, and the staff rota to model the new timetable

BUILDING BLOCKS: title, exec summary (reused as section tracker), decision slides, appendix
ASSUMPTIONS / GAPS: Assumed one staff member can support ~55 visitors an hour (please confirm).
Assumed staff contracts allow the two daytime shifts to move to 3pm–9pm.
```
