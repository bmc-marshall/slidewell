# Storytelling and structure

**Covers:** the principles of what a deck says: audience, the pyramid principle, grouping and order, SCQA openings, deck types, headline tests, planning, and standalone vs voice-over decks.

**Doesn't cover:** worked examples and the storyline proposal format (`storyline-examples.md`).

Read this before drafting a storyline or any headline. Structural errors are a bigger barrier to communication than style, and they are cheaper to fix before slides exist than after. Agree the story first; design second.

## 1. Understand the audience

Before structuring anything, establish four things. If the user hasn't said, ask.

| Question | What to find out | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Position** | Are they positive, neutral, or negative toward the message? | A skeptical audience needs evidence before the ask; a supportive one can take the answer up front with lighter support. |
| **Interests** | What do they already know? How much do they care? | Sets how much context the Situation needs and which evidence will land. |
| **Preferred style** | Detail vs summary; analysis vs recommendation | Sets slide density and whether to lead with findings or with the decision. |
| **Expectations** | Update, prep for execs, knowledge transfer, workshop participation | Sets the deck's job and how each slide should be used. |

The first question underneath all of these: **what is the successful outcome this deck should produce?**

## 2. The Pyramid Principle

Organize ideas top-down so the audience grasps them quickly and is led toward action.

1. **Start with the key message**: the main answer, recommendation, or request (the thesis). This feels counterintuitive to people trained to reason inductively, but it maximizes time spent on the important issue, avoids unnecessary detail, works with senior audiences who think top-down, and sounds clear and confident.
2. **Follow with grouped supporting arguments**: the high-level reasons that justify the thesis. Audiences group information naturally anyway; doing it for them is easier and more impactful (the rule of three often works well).
3. **Then order the supporting evidence**: the individual facts and commentary beneath each argument.

Ideas are *presented* top-down but usually *developed* bottom-up: turn raw ideas into sentences, group similar sentences, then summarize each group upward until one thesis remains.

In a deck, the levels map like this:
- Thesis → title slide and executive summary
- Supporting arguments → sections (with section dividers or breadcrumbs)
- Evidence → individual slides, each with its own mini-pyramid (headline → grouped support)

**Why sequence matters:** readers take in one sentence at a time and assume ideas that appear together belong together. If the relationship isn't stated, they will invent one and may reach a different conclusion. Controlling the sequence of ideas is the single most important act in clear communication.

## 3. Grouping, MECE, and order

Three tests apply to every grouping, at every level of the pyramid.

**Grouping / abstracting.** An idea at any level must summarize the ideas grouped below it. Apples + pears = fruit; chairs + tables = furniture; apples + tables = "objects" (technically true, useless). Items in a group must be the same kind of idea and at the same level of detail. Common contaminants are an *enabler* mixed in with reasons ("HR put fruit bowls on each floor" is not a reason to eat fruit) or a *comment* mixed in with strategies ("the programme has important implications" is not a strategy).

**MECE (mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive).** No overlap between arguments (avoid double counting) and no gaps (nothing important left out). Check each group for repetition (e.g., "the kitchen is too small" repeating "we need more room") and for missing pieces.

**Order.** The order should reflect the logic of the content, so the audience can predict what comes next. Choose deliberately and keep it consistent within a group.

| Order | Use when | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **By importance** (comparative) | Items compete for attention; the audience may stop early | Biggest saving first; most important recommendation first |
| **Chronological / sequential** | Steps, phases, history, cause → effect | Six planning questions in sequence; past → present → future; 2019 → 2023 → 2026 |
| **Short term → long term** | Recommendations or actions with different time horizons | Quick front-of-house fixes, then longer-term gallery changes; phases 1–2 now, phase 3 later |
| **Strategy → operations** (why → what → how) | Moving from direction to delivery | Vision and priorities → operating model → workforce plan → next steps |
| **Internal → external** (or the reverse) | Analysing an organisation and its environment | SWOT: strengths and weaknesses (internal), then opportunities and threats (external); team → partners → public |
| **Geographic / structural** (local → regional → national; parts of a whole) | Places, units, or components | Local partnerships → regional coalitions → national associations; UK, France, Germany |
| **Deductive** (premise → premise → conclusion) | Building a logical case | Demand has moved → staffing hasn't → so we should move staff |
| **Problem → solution → ask** | Persuading someone to act | The investment pitch (storyline-examples, Example A) |
| **General → specific** | Teaching or explaining a framework, then applying it | What strategic planning is → how to do it → what it means for us |

When a group has no natural order, use importance. When two orders compete (e.g., chronology vs. importance), choose the one that serves the headline: if the headline is about what matters most, order by importance; if it's about how something unfolds, order by time.

Grouping errors cascade. If ideas in a group are not the same kind, it becomes impossible to order them or to check whether they are MECE. Fix grouping first.

**Quick grouping check** (run it on every section, every slide's points, and every set of boxes or bullets):
- **Same kind of idea:** reasons with reasons, actions with actions. Don't slip an enabler into a list of reasons, or a comment into a list of strategies.
- **Same level of detail:** "Asia, Europe, Germany" mixes levels.
- **Summarized by its parent:** the section name or headline must be a true summary of everything beneath it.
- **MECE:** no overlaps (nothing counted twice) and no gaps (nothing important missing).
- **Then order:** if the items aren't the same kind of idea, no order will make sense.

## 4. The introductory flow (SCR / SCQA)

Logic is not enough; the audience also needs a story. Use **Situation → Complication → Question → Answer** to open a deck (or a section, an email, a single slide).

| Element | What it does | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Situation** | State relevant facts that are not in dispute | Gets everyone on the same page; shows you know what matters to them; builds buy-in |
| **Complication** | State the problem that justifies communicating | Builds positive tension; sets the audience up to expect action; creates shared purpose |
| **Question** | Frame the question the deck will answer (often implicit) | Cues up the answer; acts as a page-turner |
| **Answer** | The thesis, i.e., the top of the pyramid | Everything after this is support |

In a deck this often maps to: slide 1 = Situation, slide 2 = Complication, slide 3 = main point (Answer), following slides = supporting points. In shorter decks, S, C, and Q can share the executive summary.

**Risks to check:**
- *The situation is itself a complication*: the audience doesn't accept the starting context ("We all agree that…" when they don't).
- *The complication is divisive*: people care about it to different degrees, so some disengage.
- *The tone reads as blame*: "you should have solved this weeks ago" makes people defensive and stop listening.
- *No solution follows*: the structure collapses into an open-ended "we need to come up with a solution."

## 5. Common SCQ patterns

Use these to identify which kind of communication the deck is, then shape S, C, and Q accordingly.

| Type | Goal | Situation / Complication / Question |
|---|---|---|
| **Directive** | Tell someone to do something | S: We want to do X. C: We need you to do Y. Q: How do I do Y? |
| **Request for funds or approval** | A simple yes/no decision | S: Team wants to buy X. C: They can't without approval. Q: Should I approve it? |
| **"How to"** | Solve a problem | S: Your current system is X. C: It doesn't work properly. Q: How do we change it to make it work? |
| **Proposal** | Win work or support | S: We have a problem. C: We've decided to bring in help. Q: Are you the right people? |
| **Progress review** | Status update | S: You have problem X. C: We said we'd do Y; we've done Y. Q: What did you find? |
| **Workshop** | Get a group to think, decide, or build something together | S: Where we are and why we're here. C: What's unresolved or at stake. Q: What do we need to leave this session with? |

## 5b. Deck types

Findings, recommendation, options-appraisal, update, and proposal decks share one visual system, but they answer different questions, so their storylines and building blocks differ. Identify the type at intake (Q3); the section menu in `intake.md` pre-ticks the building blocks for each. Many real decks mix types (findings that end in a recommendation); pick the type that matches the *governing message*.

| Type | Answers | Governing message looks like | Typical arc | Signature slides |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Findings** | What did you find? | "Demand has moved to evenings, and our staffing hasn't followed" | Exec summary of findings → one section per finding (evidence slides) → implications → next steps | Exec summary; chart + takeaway slides; implications |
| **Recommendation** | What should we do? | "We recommend a six-store pilot from March" | Answer first → why (2–4 reasons) → how (plan, cost, timeline) → risks → the ask | Exec summary; show-the-math; timeline; risks; decision bar |
| **Options appraisal** | Which option should we choose? | "Option B gives the most value for the least risk" | The decision and criteria → long list → short list → assessment against criteria → preferred option → what it takes → the ask | Criteria; long list → short list; options matrix (labelled RAG); preferred option; decision bar |
| **Update / progress** | Where are we, and what do you need to decide? | "We're on track for go-live; two decisions are needed today" | Status at a glance → progress against plan → issues and risks → decisions needed → next steps | Agenda of standing items; RAG status summary; milestone timeline; risk/issue log; decisions-needed slide |
| **Proposal** (pitching for work) | Why should you choose us, and what exactly would you get? | "We'd help you build a growth plan in 8 weeks, with optional extensions to June" | Our understanding of your goals → proposed approach (core + options) → how we'd deliver (plan) → commercials → what you'd get (deliverables) → why us (team, credentials, case studies) → next steps | Contents; understanding; phased approach; Gantt; pricing options; deliverable thumbnails; team with headshots; impact numbers; logo wall; case studies; client quote |
| **Workshop** | What will we leave this session with? | "Today we'll agree where the journey breaks and pick three fixes" | Objectives and agenda → warm-up → teach → exercises and capture → decide → objectives revisited | Objectives; agenda with times; norms; exercise and capture slides; questions to the room |

Notes:
- **Agendas belong to workshops and update decks.** In findings, recommendation, and options decks, the executive summary *is* the map of the deck; don't add a separate agenda (reuse the summary as a "Contents" tracker instead).
- **Update decks** may use short topic labels for standing items ("Budget", "Recruitment") because the audience expects the same structure every time, but every item that needs discussion or a decision still gets a message headline, and the deck opens with a status summary, not a list of topics.
- **Proposals** lead with the client's goals, not with your firm: credentials and case studies come after the approach, as evidence you can deliver it. Offer a core scope with optional extensions so the client can say yes to something.
- **Options appraisals** state the criteria before scoring anything, and show every short-listed option assessed the same way (same criteria, same order, same rating scale).

## 6. Headlines

Every content slide has a headline that:
- **States a message, not a topic.** Avoid "intellectually blank statements" (IBS): "Outlook" or "Traffic data" says nothing; "The outlook is positive" or "Three of four corridors exceed 13,000 vehicles a day" does.
- **Makes exactly one point**, and that point is supported by everything on the slide (vertical logic).
- **Is two lines or shorter.**
- **Makes the reader want to read on.**
- **Fits the sequence (horizontal logic).** Reading only the headlines, in order, should tell the whole story. Test this explicitly before building slides.

A synthesis headline fails if it isn't supported by the content below it (wrong), draws on only some of the content (too narrow), or reaches beyond it (too wide).

Section dividers, agenda trackers, and exercise slides may use shorter labels rather than full message headlines, because their job is navigation or instruction rather than argument.

## 7. Planning the deck

It is far easier to add and tweak content once the whole story is mapped. Work in this order and get user agreement at the storyline stage before building:

1. **Plan**: set the communication strategy from the aims and the audience.
2. **Outline**: write down what needs to be delivered, as a story.
3. **Structure**: arrange the messages as a pyramid.
4. **Storyboard**: one line per slide: headline + what will prove it (chart, table, framework, exercise).
5. **Ghost deck**: build slides with real headlines and placeholder content, then iterate slide by slide.

Signpost throughout: tell them what you're going to tell them, tell them, then tell them what you told them. This is why agenda trackers, breadcrumbs, and recaps exist.

Know when to stop polishing. Every communication can be improved, but direction matters more than detail: a deck with the right story and adequate formatting beats a beautifully formatted deck with the wrong story.

## 8. Standalone vs voice-over

Decide before building which kind of slide this is, because it changes how much a slide must carry.

- **Standalone** (the deck will be emailed or read without a presenter): every slide must fully function without narration. Headlines are complete messages, evidence is labeled and sourced, and takeaways are stated on the slide.
- **Voice-over** (live presentation or workshop): slides can be sparser and support what the presenter says, but plan how each one will be used, i.e., what the presenter says, asks, or has the audience do while it's on screen.

Make it clear what the audience is expected to do with each slide: absorb it, discuss it, brainstorm, or ask questions.

## 9. Common failures

When reviewing a storyline, look for:
1. **Failure to group logically**: ideas not the same kind (e.g., "Asia, Africa, Angola", or mixing actions with situations), not in a logical order, or not MECE.
2. **Failure to synthesize**: IBS headlines instead of conclusions; conclusions not supported by, too narrow for, or too wide for the content below them.
3. **Failure to contextualize**: no story. The material is correct but the audience isn't told why it matters or what is being asked of them.
