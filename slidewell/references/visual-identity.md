# Visual identity: palettes, brand motifs, and animation

**Covers:** the deck's overall look, decided once at the theme step: building from the brand, palette strategies, brand motifs, typography, the shape vocabulary, engaging spoken decks, and animation.

**Doesn't cover:** slide-level finishing rules such as colour roles, corners, and emphasis (`finishing-standard.md`).

Read this at the theme step (SKILL.md §A3) whenever there is no template to follow, and whenever the user wants a deck that feels native to a client or product. The aim is a deck that looks like it *belongs* to the organization it's about, without decoration that competes with the content.

## 1. Build the identity from the subject's brand

Work through these in order and write the results into the theme spec:

1. **Colours from the logo and brand assets.** Sample the logo's colours, then choose a palette strategy (§2). The logo's dominant colour usually becomes the headline colour.
2. **A motif, if the brand offers one** (§3): a simple geometric feature of the logo or product that can become a frame, border, or bullet.
3. **Typography** (§4): a clean body font, plus a display font echoing the brand's lettering where that helps.
4. **Imagery:** full-bleed photos on the title and closing slides (and optionally section dividers), with the brand colour or motif overlaid. Content slides stay clean.
5. **Bookends:** the title and closing slides use the same design device (e.g., the same photo treatment and frame), so the deck visibly opens and closes as one piece.

Only use logos and brand marks the user supplies or confirms they may use. Never distort, recolour, or crop a logo.

## 2. Palette strategies

All work within the ≤6-colour rule (finishing-standard §3). Pick one strategy and apply it consistently: the same colour always means the same thing.

| Strategy | What it is | When it works | Examples seen |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Shades + complementary accent** | Two or three shades of the brand hue for structure, plus one accent from the opposite side of the colour wheel for emphasis | The default for professional findings decks: calm, branded, with one colour that pops | An aerospace recommendation deck: deep brand blue (headlines, bars), a lighter blue (breadcrumbs, secondary), and an orange-red accent. A club proposal: dark, mid, and pale greens with the same orange-red accent |
| **Monochrome shades** | One hue in dark, mid, and light tints, plus greys | Data-heavy decks where charts need several related colours without visual noise | A robotics investment deck in dark and pale greens; stacked bars in two shades of one green |
| **Dual brand colours** | Two strong brand colours used together | When the brand itself is defined by a pair | An energy-drink strategy slide in brand navy and red; a construction-equipment template in logo red and yellow with greys |
| **Brand group (multi-hue)** | Several distinct hues, each with a fixed meaning | When categories need their own identity (e.g., four brand values, several stakeholder groups), or the client's brand is playful | A theatre deck in burgundy, forest green, and teal; a healthcare client deck giving each of four values its own colour |
| **Minimalist single colour** | One strong colour plus black, white, and greys | Workshops and talks where slides should feel bold and simple; the colour signals importance or interaction | A strategic-planning workshop in one red with charcoal text and grey panels |

Rules of thumb:
- **One main colour** carries headlines and primary emphasis; the accent is reserved for the single thing the slide wants noticed.
- **Greys do the quiet work:** secondary text, muted bars, inactive tracker rows, panels.
- **Complementary accents** (opposite on the colour wheel: blue ↔ orange, green ↔ red-orange, purple ↔ yellow) give the strongest emphasis with the fewest colours.
- **Check contrast:** body text on coloured fills must stay readable; light tints carry dark text, dark fills carry white text.
- **Charts inherit the palette:** focal series in the main or accent colour, everything else in greys or tints (charts.md §5).

## 3. Brand motifs: turning a logo feature into a design element

When the logo or product has a distinctive, simple shape, take that feature and build it into the slide geometry. It makes the deck feel made for the client rather than dressed in their colours. Examples of the technique:
- **Corner brackets from a logo:** a logo containing a right-angle element becomes corner brackets in the top-right and bottom-left of content slides, and frames photos. On the title slide a white bracket frames the title, setting up the device, and the closing slide repeats it.
- **Borders from a product:** a can's design (a coloured band and edge) becomes a left-hand and bottom border on the slide.
- **Icons as bullets:** the company's small symbol replaces the standard bullet character.
- **Lettering style:** a logo set in bold slanted type inspires slanted, boxed numerals for numbered takeaways and section numbers.
- **Edges, not just shapes:** a motif can be the *edge* where a colour panel meets a photo: a wavy or organic edge echoing the brand (a drip, a wave, a torn edge), a slanted cut, or thin curved lines sweeping past a cropped image. Use the same edge on every divider and on the title and closing slides.
- **A recurring hero image:** one strong photo (a symbol of the cause, the product) reused on every section divider, cropped the same way each time, so the image itself becomes the section marker.

How to find a motif:
1. Ask the user whether the brand has a motif they like, and look at the logo, product, and the organisation's website for candidate features (a corner, a curve, a slant, a shape, a lettering style, a pattern).
2. Pick two or three candidates and **mock each one up** on a title slide and one content slide (render them as images side by side), so the user can choose by eye. A motif is hard to judge in words.
3. Use the chosen motif; if none suits, use no motif (a clean palette and typography are enough).

How to apply a motif:
- **Use it enough to register:** in at least three consistent roles, e.g. (1) large on the title and closing slides, (2) a small recurring element on every content slide (a corner, an edge, a header tab), and (3) as the marker of the *current* item on trackers or section dividers. Used once, it reads as decoration; used consistently, it becomes the deck's signature.
- Use it **only if the brand genuinely offers one**: a clean geometric element that survives being simplified. Don't force a motif onto a brand without one.
- Keep it **sparing and fixed in position**: the same corners or edges on every slide, drawn from the theme spec like any recurring element.
- **Bookend with it:** introduce it on the title slide and close with it, so it reads as intentional.
- It must **never compete with content** or crowd the margins (keep the outer-margin rules).
- Build it from native shapes so the user can edit it.

## 4. Typography from the brand

- **Body font:** a clean, highly legible sans serif, used for nearly everything.
- **Display font (optional, used sparingly):** where the brand's own lettering is distinctive (e.g., a serif or ornate wordmark), echo it with a matching display font, best kept to the title and closing slides or a highlighted quote. Within content slides, keep headlines in the body font so each slide reads as one consistent typeface.
- Maximum two fonts (finishing-standard §2). Check the fonts are available on the presenting machine (SKILL.md §A3); if not, choose close, widely available alternatives and say so.

## 4b. A consistent shape vocabulary and small enrichments

A deck feels designed when every visual device has one job and is drawn the same way every time. An energy-economics class deck (dense because of its short slot, but rich in technique) shows the ideas:

**One device, one job, one style.**
- **Title tag:** the breadcrumb sits in a dark tab flush with the top-left edge, with a straight slanted right end (not a pointed chevron) and white caps text, left-aligned. It's sleek, and it's the same size and position on every slide.
- **Highlight pill:** key phrases inside bullets are set in white bold text on a dark pill, the same style as the title tag, so "dark fill + white text" consistently means *this matters*.
- **Circles:** every circle uses the same inner-and-outer ring combination, whether it holds an icon or a date.
- **Callouts:** one callout style, one box size, one font size, used throughout.
- **Rating markers:** small circled up / neutral / down arrows with a three-item legend (High / Mid / Low) rate each panel of a framework at a glance.

Decide the vocabulary in the theme spec (which shape means what, in which size and style) and draw every instance from it.

**Order inside busy slides.** A world map with ten callouts stays readable because every callout box sits on a rectangular grid around the map's edge, the boxes are equally spaced and the same size, leader lines run to each location, and the fill colour encodes one thing (growing / mixed / declining). Busy content needs *more* alignment, not less.

**Repeat a geometric idea.** The slant of the title tab reappears in the slanted divider between two panels ("Non-issues" vs "Risks and issues"), in the diagonal photo cut on the title slide, and in a timeline whose dates step down a slanted line. One idea, repeated, ties the deck together.

**Keep connectors visible across backgrounds.** When an arrow or line crosses from a dark panel onto a white background, give it two tones (light on the dark part, dark on the white part) so it reads along its whole length.

**Small enrichments.** A slanted timeline, a subtle icon, or a photo crop can make a slide more interesting without adding content. Add them only after the slide works without them.

**Easter eggs.** A tiny recurring hidden motif (e.g., a small character tucked into a corner on several slides) can reward an audience in a relaxed setting such as a class or internal session. Use rarely, never in formal client or board decks, and only if the user asks or the setting clearly allows it.

**Highlight pills behind running text.** Use them to bring out a specific phrase inside *longer* text, such as a quote or a dense paragraph. For short bullets, **bold** the lead-in instead (optionally in a brand highlight colour, as long as it can't be confused with a nearby chart's colours). Build pills the way a designer does: the text stays in its normal text box, and a filled rectangle sits behind the highlighted words. (Putting the phrase in its own filled box tends to wrap early or force awkward spacing, so don't.) This depends on the text staying exactly where it is, so:
- Use it only when the deck's font is available both where you render and check the slides and on the user's machine. Then the render matches what they'll see and the pills can be positioned and verified precisely. If the font is being substituted in your renders, say so and don't claim the pills are aligned.
- Position each pill from the measured text and check every one on the rendered slide.
- In the delivery note, tell the user that highlights are separate shapes behind the text: if they edit the wording or change the font, they'll need to nudge the pills too.

**Icons.** Use one icon set in one style (all outline or all solid), one stroke weight, and one colour, either plain or in identical solid circles. Give each icon one meaning and keep it (the same "search" icon always means research). Use icons as column or section markers, not as decoration on every bullet. `slidekit.icon()` draws from the bundled open-licence set (Lucide, ISC licence); the names are listed in `assets/icons.txt`.

## 4c. Imagery

Photos make a deck feel human and specific, but only if each one has a job and the text on or near it stays legible.

**Where photos go (roles)**
- **Title, section divider, and closing slides:** full-bleed or half-bleed (photo on one side, brand colour on the other, divided by the deck's edge motif).
- **Side panels** on content slides: a photo strip down one side, about a quarter to a third of the width, beside the content; or a photo panel carrying a short statement over an overlay.
- **Mosaics:** a grid of photos mixed with brand-colour tiles carrying one word each (a mood board, the stages of an offer).
- **People slides:** headshots cropped to the same shape and size (circles or squares), with name, role, and a one-line bio.
- **Shaped crops** (a circle, a quarter-circle into the corner, an edge-motif cut) as a motif, used consistently.

**Choosing photos**
- Match the audience and subject: show the people the deck is about (the target consumers, the beneficiaries, the staff), doing what the deck is about.
- One photographic style per deck (colour treatment, framing, warmth). Mixed styles look like stock picked at random.
- Every photo must be one the user supplied or has rights to use. Claude can't download stock photos here, and must never use a photo of a real public figure found on the web.

**Text on photos** *(checked: `check_deck.py` measures the contrast of the pixels under the text)*
- Never put text straight onto a busy or mid-tone photo. Use one of:
  - a **gradient fade** from about 80% dark (or brand colour) under the text to clear over the subject, so the photo still shows (`slidekit.overlay(mode="gradient")`);
  - a **semi-transparent panel** in a brand colour or near-black (60–85% opacity) behind the text block (`overlay(mode="panel")`);
  - a photo that is already dark or light where the text sits.
- Position the text over the calm part of the image, and the fade or panel on the opposite side from the subject's face.

**When the user hasn't supplied photos**
Build the layout anyway with **placeholder frames** (`slidekit.photo(...)` without a file): grey frames at exactly the final size and crop, each labelled with the photo it needs ("[Photo: volunteers at a stall, smiling, outdoors]"), with any overlay already in place. List the photos needed in the delivery note so the user can drop them in.

**Suggest a real photo for every placeholder.** When the style calls for photos and the user has none, search Unsplash for each slot (web search with `site:unsplash.com/photos` and a description of the shot; a stock site with a free licence, Unsplash first) and pick one whose subject, mood, and orientation fit the frame and leave room for any text or overlay. Pass its page URL as `slidekit.photo(..., link=url)`: the placeholder then shows a short clickable link under its label, and the full URL is in its alt text. Repeat the links in the delivery note as a list by slide. Prefer free photos: skip any marked Unsplash+ (paid), and tell the user to confirm the licence when they download. If downloading works in your environment, download the photo and place it with `path=` and `focus=` instead, cropped so the subject sits where the layout needs it. *Why:* a placeholder with a named, fitting photo turns "find a picture" from a chore into one click, and shows the user what the slide is meant to look like.

**Logos**
- **Placement is a choice:** ask (intake Q8c). Common options: a co-branded lockup bottom-left on every slide (client logo, a thin divider, then your own), a single logo top-right, or logos only on the title and closing slides.
- **Co-branding never competes:** both logos at the same visual height, aligned on one baseline, with clear space between them; the client's logo first when the deck is for the client.
- **Logo walls** (clients, partners, funders): every logo scaled to the same visual weight, in a regular grid, all in full colour or all in one tone.
- Use logos only if the user supplies them or confirms they may be used.

## 5. Engaging, spoken decks

Some decks are meant to be *talked through*: a briefing, a teaching session, a talk. They follow the same rules but use them differently. An explainer on a national health system, delivered to an audience from another country, showed the techniques:
- **Conversational headlines in the header bar:** "Why should we think about other healthcare systems?", "What *is* the health service?", "Yes… but what *is* it?". Questions are fine here (storyline-examples §2) because the speaker answers them aloud.
- **Humour with a purpose:** definitions appear and are crossed out one by one, ending with a full-slide punchline ("It's complicated!") made of the real organisational maps. The joke *is* the point: the system is complex.
- **Pacing devices:** "Half time" and "Question time" dividers, numbered takeaway tiles ("What should you take away from this?"), and a short "Otherwise… we're under water" consequences slide.
- **Sparse main deck, rich back half:** the spoken section has little text per slide; the detailed reference material (structure, history, funding, comparisons, resources) sits after the talk as a readable pack.
- **Brand carried lightly:** brand blue header bars, a slanted numeral style echoing the logo, and one big logo slide as the reveal.

## 6. Animation and reveals

**Ask first** (intake Q3, in the first round): will the deck be animated? The likely answer depends on how it will be used. A deck that will be **presented live** is a good candidate for animation; a deck that will be **sent as an attachment, read ahead, or printed** should not rely on it, because a reader scrolling the file or a PDF sees only the final state.

**Which style:**
- **Not animated (shared, printed, or the user said no):** show builds as successive slides (n/N), with earlier items faded along their own hue (layout-patterns §G). Every state is visible, printable, and editable.
- **Animated, items of equal weight (the default when animating):** each item fades in on its own click and stays at full strength (`slidekit.animate_on_click(slide, groups)`). Use for lists, tiles, options, or steps that sit side by side as equals.
- **Animated, the newest item matters most:** each item fades in and the previous one dims (`animate_on_click(..., dim_previous=True)`). Use when later content supersedes or refines earlier content and the audience should focus only on the latest: how thinking evolved to the current view, a sequence where each step replaces the last, or anything where the right-most or bottom-most item has priority.
- **Transitions between slides:** none, or one subtle style for the whole deck (fade; push only for a deck that moves strictly forward through stages), never a mix (`slidekit.transition()`; checked). **Entrance effects:** fade by default; wipe only for things that grow, like a bar or a line.
- Whatever the style, also produce a **static share version** with every element visible in its final state (SKILL.md mode D) if the deck will be circulated afterwards.

When the user wants a live, animated version, the explainer deck shows how animation should be used. Its animations (read from the file):
- **Contrast reveal:** a photo with four strengths of a healthcare system; on click, the strengths slide up (motion path) and four matching weaknesses fade in beneath them.
- **Punchline footnote:** a provocative claim, then the source footnote appears on click.
- **Cumulative cross-out:** four attempted definitions appear one per click, each crossed out, followed by an ellipsis that grows, then the "It's complicated!" slide where images zoom in one after another.
- **One idea per click:** almost every effect is a simple Appear or Fade triggered by a click; there are no transitions and no decorative motion.

Rules distilled:
1. Animate to **reduce text on screen and control pacing**, not to decorate.
2. **One idea per click**, in the order you'll speak about it.
3. Use **Appear or Fade**; reserve motion for meaningful change (moving items to make room for their counterparts).
4. **Build to a point:** reveals should accumulate towards a conclusion or punchline.
5. Always produce a **static share version** in which every element is visible in its final state (SKILL.md mode D).
6. Build each element to be animated as a separate object (or a list of objects that appear together), add the animation with `slidekit.animate_on_click`, and list the click sequence in the delivery note (slide, click, what appears) so the user can check it. Renders and PDFs show only the final state, so ask the user to run the animated slides in Slide Show before presenting.
